Think like a data detective

Four questions, in order. Drift answers each one from the live NASA data.

Step 1

What is changing?

Pick a variable and season. The headline tells you the global trend and how much of the surface changed.

Step 2

Where?

Read the map. Strong colours are robust trends; faded cells could be noise.

Step 3

How much?

Click a place or drag a box. The panel gives the rate per decade and the total change over your window.

Step 4

Is it significant?

Check the p-value and the confidence band. Then ask what could explain it. Drift can't tell you the cause.

Variables you can explore

Surface temperature anomaly

How much warmer or cooler a year was than normal.

NASA's GISTEMP record compares each month with the 1951–1980 average for the same place. A value of +1 °C means one degree warmer than that baseline. Using anomalies lets Drift compare the poles with the tropics fairly.

Precipitation rate

How much rain and snow falls, as millimetres of water per day.

GPCP blends rain gauges with satellite observations into a global grid. In Drift you can also view trends as a percentage of a place's normal rainfall, which makes deserts and rainforests comparable.

Seasons

Trends can differ between winter and summer.

Choose Dec–Feb, Mar–May, Jun–Aug or Sep–Nov to test one season. Drift only uses a season when all three of its months have data, so nothing is filled in or guessed.

Grid cells

The planet is split into small boxes, and each one gets its own test.

GISTEMP cells are 2° wide and GPCP cells are 2.5°. Drift runs about 16,000 separate trend tests every time you change a setting, using your computer's own processor.

Reading a trend

Trend

A steady direction over many years: rising, falling or flat.

Single years jump around because of weather and cycles like El Niño. A trend is the slow drift underneath. Drift reports it per decade so different periods are easy to compare.

Sen's slope

The middle value of the slopes between every pair of years.

For 45 years there are 990 pairs. Taking their median means one freak year can't drag the answer around, which makes it more trustworthy than a simple straight-line fit for noisy climate data.

95% confidence band

The range of slopes that fit the data well.

If the whole band is above zero, the place is clearly warming or getting wetter. A wide band means the record is too noisy or too short to be sure of the exact rate.

Change point

The year a record seems to jump to a new level.

The Pettitt test finds the most likely break. A sudden shift, like an instrument change or a regime change in the ocean, can look like a trend, so Drift shows both.

Is it real or just noise?

Mann–Kendall test

Counts how often later years are higher than earlier ones.

If rises and falls were random, they'd roughly cancel out. A large surplus in one direction is evidence of a real trend. It works on ranks, so it doesn't assume the data follow a neat bell curve.

p-value

How surprising the result would be if nothing were really changing.

p = 0.01 means a pattern this strong would turn up by chance only about 1 time in 100. Smaller p means stronger evidence. It does not tell you why something changed.

Persistence (autocorrelation)

Neighbouring years tend to be alike, so the data hold less information than they seem to.

A warm year is often followed by another warm year. Ignoring this makes trends look more certain than they are. Drift corrects for it with the Hamed–Rao method, which counts the effective number of independent years.

False discovery rate

When you run thousands of tests, some will pass by luck.

At a 5% threshold, 16,000 tests would give about 800 false alarms. Drift uses the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to keep false alarms to about 10% of the cells it calls robust. That's why some cells fade.

What a trend can't tell you

Cause

Robust ≠ explained

A significant trend means the change is unlikely to be chance. Finding out why needs physics, models and other evidence.

Window

Years matter

Short windows are dominated by cycles like El Niño. Try 30 years or more, and see how the answer moves when you shift the window.

Coverage

Early data is sparse

Before about 1950 there were few weather stations in many regions. Drift leaves cells blank when data is missing in more than 30% of the years.
